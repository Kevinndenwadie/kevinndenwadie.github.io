## Working Papers
<ins>Informality and Monetary Policy Revisited**(Job Market Paper)**</ins>
>**Abstract:** Standard New Keynesian models with search frictions treat job destruction as exogenous and constant, implying that monetary policy affects employment mainly through hiring. This assumption is particularly restrictive in EMDEs with a large informal economy, as it rules out the very adjustment margin that informality provides. Motivated by empirical evidence from a structural VAR for Mexico, I develop a New Keynesian model with a dual labor market. The formal sector features search-and-matching frictions, staggered nominal wage contracts subject to downward nominal wage rigidity (DNWR), and match-specific idiosyncratic productivity, so that matches are severed when productivity falls below a reservation threshold. The informal sector is competitive with fully flexible wages. I show that endogenizing the job separation decision creates a new channel through which informality and monetary policy interact: the reservation productivity threshold depends on the formal-informal real wage differential so that the informal economy shapes the value of a formal match and the cutoff below which matches are destroyed. Following a contractionary monetary policy shock, DNWR prevents formal nominal wages from adjusting downward, which raises the threshold. Formal job losses are then 2.2 times higher than in a model with a constant separation rate. DNWR on its own introduces an amplification mecanism as the cost of disinflatiion is 2.4 times higher under rigid than under flexible nominal wages. Finally, informality facilitates inflation stability at the cost of a higher sacrifice ratio, making monetary policy less effective.

<ins>Exogenous Shocks, Households Heterogeneity and Informality</ins>
>In this paper, I investigate the importance of households heterogeneity in understanding the distributional impacts of macroeconomic shocks on the size of the informal economy and on households’ welfare costs of external fluctuations. Using a two-sector search and matching framework enriched with a Real Business Cycle approach, I compare outcomes from a representative household model to one that accounts for households heterogeneity, both calibrated to Mexico. The findings reveal that the representative household framework may lead to misguided policy prescriptions for improving welfare and reducing informality. It overestimates the informal economy’s reaction to external shocks and fails to capture the vulnerability of lower-income households, as it behaves more like a wealthier household in the two-agent framework. The paper advocates for moving beyond representative household models and adopting heterogeneous models based on microdata that better reflect the complexities of labor markets in emerging economies, thus supporting the design of more inclusive welfare and labor market policies especially in high informality context.
